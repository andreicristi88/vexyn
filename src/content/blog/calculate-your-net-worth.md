Income tells you what flows through your hands. Net worth tells you what you have actually kept. It is one number — everything you own minus everything you owe — and it is the closest thing personal finance has to a scoreboard. This guide explains how to calculate it properly, what belongs on each side, and how to watch it move over time, using a calculator that keeps every figure on your own device.

## Before you start — the one formula

Net worth is simple arithmetic that people overcomplicate:

**Net worth = total assets − total liabilities.**

Assets are what you own that has value. Liabilities are what you owe. Subtract the second from the first and you have your net worth. It can be negative — common early in life when student loans outweigh savings — and that is information, not a failure. What matters is the direction it moves over time.

To do this well you need current balances, not estimates. Log into each account and note today's figure rather than guessing from memory, which is almost always optimistic on the asset side and forgetful on the liability side.

## Add up what you own

Gather the current value of everything on the asset side:

- **Cash and bank balances** — checking, savings, and any cash you actually hold.
- **Investments** — brokerage accounts, index funds, pensions and retirement accounts at their current value, not what you paid in.
- **Property** — the realistic market value of a home you own, not the purchase price and not the mortgage balance (that goes on the other side).
- **Vehicles and valuables** — a car, and anything else genuinely worth a meaningful amount and sellable.

Be honest and conservative on value. A house is worth what it would sell for today, minus what selling costs; a five-year-old car is worth its resale price, not its showroom price. Overstating assets just gives you a comforting number that means nothing.

## Add up what you owe

Now the liability side — the outstanding balance of everything you owe:

- **Credit cards** — the current balance, not the limit.
- **Loans** — student loans, car finance, personal loans, buy-now-pay-later balances.
- **Mortgage** — the remaining balance on the loan, which is why the home's full value sits on the asset side.

Enter the amount you would need to pay to clear each one today. The gap between a home's value and its mortgage — your equity — falls out of the calculation naturally once both sides are entered.

## Track it over time

A single net worth figure is a snapshot; the value is in the trend. Calculate it on the same day each month — the first, say — and note the number. Over a year the line tells you more than any single month can: whether saving and paying down debt are actually outrunning spending. Do not obsess over month-to-month wobble, especially if you hold investments whose value moves with the market. The slope over quarters is the signal.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Using the purchase price of assets.** A home or car is worth what it would sell for now, not what you paid. Investments are worth their current value, not your contributions.
- **Forgetting liabilities.** Buy-now-pay-later balances, a car loan you barely think about, an overdraft — leaving these off inflates the number and defeats the point.
- **Counting the same thing twice.** A home's market value goes in assets and the mortgage balance in liabilities; do not net them yourself and also enter them separately.
- **Chasing monthly noise.** If you hold investments, the figure moves with markets. Track the trend across quarters, not the wobble between two months.
- **Storing it somewhere public.** Your full financial position is not something to paste into a random online tool. Keep it local.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is net worth, exactly?

Net worth is everything you own (assets) minus everything you owe (liabilities). It is a snapshot of your financial position at a moment in time, and it can be positive or negative.

### What should I include as an asset?

Cash and bank balances, investments and pensions at current value, the market value of property you own, and vehicles or valuables worth a meaningful, sellable amount. Value them at what they would fetch today, conservatively.

### Should my mortgage reduce my house's value?

No — enter the home's full market value as an asset and the remaining mortgage as a separate liability. The calculation nets them for you, and the difference is your equity.

### How often should I calculate my net worth?

Once a month, on the same day, is a good rhythm. Any more often and market noise drowns the signal; any less and you lose the trend. It is the direction over time that matters.

### What if my net worth is negative?

That is common, especially with student loans or early in a mortgage, and it is not a verdict on you. Track whether it is rising over time — the trend is what counts, not the sign in a single month.

## Glossary

**Net worth** — Total assets minus total liabilities. The single figure that summarises your financial position at a point in time.

**Asset** — Something you own that has value and could be converted to cash: bank balances, investments, property, vehicles.

**Liability** — Something you owe: credit card balances, loans, the remaining mortgage. Entered as the amount needed to clear it today.

**Equity** — The share of an asset you actually own, after debt against it. For a home, its market value minus the outstanding mortgage.
