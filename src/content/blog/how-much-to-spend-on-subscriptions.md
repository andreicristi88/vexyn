"How much should I spend on subscriptions?" is a fair question with no honest single answer. Anyone quoting you a precise percentage is guessing — the right figure depends on your income, your priorities, and what the subscriptions replace. What you can do is set a limit you can defend and measure yourself against it. This guide gives a simple framework for deciding your number, then shows how to find what you actually spend today so the comparison is real rather than a feeling.

## Before you start — why there is no universal number

Budgeting rules of thumb exist, and they are useful for structure rather than precision. The best known is the 50/30/20 split: roughly half of take-home pay for needs, a third for wants, and the rest for saving and debt. Subscriptions almost always live in the "wants" third — occasionally in "needs" when a subscription is genuinely a work tool or replaces something essential.

That framing matters more than a hard cap. A person who never eats out and watches a lot of streaming might rationally spend more on subscriptions than someone who does the opposite, and both can be fine. The number is personal; the discipline is universal.

## Set a limit you can defend

Rather than chase a percentage, decide your subscription budget with three questions:

- **What share of my "wants" money am I comfortable committing in advance?** Subscriptions are pre-committed spending — money gone before the month starts. The more of your discretionary budget is locked up this way, the less flexibility you have when something comes up.
- **Would I re-subscribe today?** For each service, imagine it lapsed and ask whether you would actively pay to restart it. The ones you would not are your budget, sitting unused.
- **What does it replace?** A subscription that genuinely replaces a more expensive habit earns its place; one that duplicates something you already have does not.

A limit built from those answers is one you will actually keep, because you built the reasoning. A borrowed percentage is one you will quietly ignore.

## Measure what you actually spend now

You cannot set a limit against a number you do not know, and almost everyone underestimates this one. Export three or more months of your bank statement as CSV and run it through the [Subscription Finder](/subscription-finder). It groups charges that recur at a steady amount and interval and totals them into a monthly and yearly figure — the real number, not the remembered one.

The yearly total is usually the one that lands. A dozen small monthly charges feel trivial one at a time and add up to a meaningful annual sum when a tool finally sums them. Everything runs in your browser; the statement is never uploaded.

## Close the gap

Now you have both numbers: the limit you decided and the reality you measured. If reality is under the limit, you are done — this was a checkup. If it is over, the "would I re-subscribe today?" test from earlier is the fastest way down: cancel the ones that fail it, downgrade the ones on a tier bigger than you use, and re-measure next month to confirm the charges actually stopped.

For the wider context — how subscriptions sit against the rest of your spending — the [Spending Analyzer](/spending-analyzer) puts them in a category breakdown next to groceries, transport and the rest.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Chasing a "correct" percentage.** There isn't one. Use a rule of thumb like 50/30/20 for structure, not as a verdict.
- **Budgeting from memory.** Everyone underestimates their subscription total. Measure it from the statement before setting a limit.
- **Ignoring annual subscriptions.** A yearly charge is easy to forget and often the largest. It counts toward your monthly budget at a twelfth of its size.
- **Cutting things you value to hit an arbitrary number.** The goal is to stop paying for what you do not use, not to punish yourself. A subscription you genuinely use is not waste.
- **Setting a limit once and never rechecking.** Subscriptions creep back. Re-measure every few months.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there a recommended percentage of income for subscriptions?

No official one. Subscriptions usually fall in the "wants" portion of a budget — the ~30% in a 50/30/20 split — but the sensible amount depends on what they replace and how much of your discretionary money you want pre-committed.

### How do I find out how much I actually spend on subscriptions?

Export a few months of your bank statement and run it through a recurring-charge finder. [Vexyn's Subscription Finder](/subscription-finder) totals them into a monthly and yearly figure in your browser, which is almost always higher than people guess.

### How do I decide which subscriptions to keep?

Use the re-subscribe test: for each service, imagine it lapsed and ask whether you would actively pay to restart it today. The ones you would not are the ones to cancel.

### Do annual subscriptions count toward a monthly budget?

Yes — divide the annual price by twelve to see its monthly weight. They are easy to forget precisely because they appear once a year, and they are often the biggest single line.

### Is it safe to analyze my statement to find subscriptions?

Yes, if the tool runs locally. Vexyn's finder processes the CSV in your browser and sends nothing, which you can confirm in the Network panel. Avoid tools that upload your statement.

## Glossary

**50/30/20 rule** — A budgeting rule of thumb: roughly 50% of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants, 20% for saving and debt. Structure, not a strict law.

**Pre-committed spending** — Money promised before the month begins, like subscriptions. The more of your budget is pre-committed, the less flexibility you have for the unexpected.

**Re-subscribe test** — A quick judgement: if this service lapsed today, would you actively pay to restart it? A "no" marks a subscription you can cut.

**Discretionary income** — What is left after essential costs; the money you actually get to allocate. Subscriptions almost always draw from here.
