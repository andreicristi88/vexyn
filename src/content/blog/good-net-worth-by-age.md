Search "average net worth by age" and you will find a hundred articles quoting confident figures. Be careful with them: the numbers vary wildly by country and year, averages are skewed hard by the very wealthy, and a single figure ignores everything about your situation. This guide takes the honest route. It explains what "good" actually means here, gives a rule of thumb you can apply to yourself, points you at the real data instead of made-up numbers, and shows how to calculate your own net worth privately.

## Before you start — why "good" is the wrong question

Net worth at a given age depends on things a benchmark cannot see: where you live and its cost, whether you have student debt or an inheritance, how long you have earned, and what you earn. Two people the same age can have wildly different, equally healthy positions. So "is my net worth good for my age?" is less useful than two better questions: **is it trending up over time?** and **is it reasonable relative to what I earn?** Both are things you control; a comparison to a stranger is not.

There is also a statistical trap. Reported *averages* are dragged upward by a small number of extremely wealthy people, so the average is almost always far higher than the *median* (the typical person in the middle). If you must compare, compare to the median, and treat any average with suspicion.

## Use a rule of thumb, not a leaderboard

One widely cited benchmark comes from the book *The Millionaire Next Door* by Thomas Stanley and William Danko. Their rough formula for an "expected" net worth is:

> expected net worth = age × pre-tax annual income ÷ 10

So a 40-year-old earning 60,000 a year would have an expected net worth around 240,000 by this rule. It is deliberately crude, and it breaks at the edges — it is meaningless for someone in their early twenties, and unkind to anyone who has only recently started earning well. Treat it as a conversation starter with yourself, not a target carved in stone. Its real value is directional: it scales with both your age and your income, which a flat "average by age" figure does not.

## Find the real numbers yourself

If you want genuine benchmarks rather than a blog's recycled figures, go to the primary sources, because they are updated, methodologically sound, and free:

- In the United States, the Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/scfindex.htm) publishes median and mean net worth broken down by age, and it is the figure serious articles are ultimately quoting.
- In the United Kingdom, the Office for National Statistics runs the Wealth and Assets Survey with comparable breakdowns.

Look up the current median for your age band there and you will have a real number rather than a guessed one. Note the date of the data, and always prefer the median over the mean for a sense of the typical person.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Comparing to the average.** Averages are inflated by the ultra-wealthy. If you compare at all, compare to the median.
- **Trusting a blog's figures over the source.** Numbers get copied, rounded and staled across articles. Go to the Federal Reserve or ONS data directly.
- **Treating a rule of thumb as a target.** The age × income ÷ 10 formula is a rough gauge, not a pass/fail line, and it is meaningless when you are young.
- **Comparing across countries.** Cost of living, debt norms and pension systems differ so much that a US figure tells a UK reader little, and vice versa.
- **Ignoring your own trend.** A single snapshot says less than the direction of travel. Rising beats high-but-falling.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good net worth for my age?

There is no universal figure, because it depends on your income, location, debt and how long you have earned. A better test is whether your net worth is rising over time and is reasonable relative to your income. For a real benchmark, look up the median for your age band from the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances or the UK's Wealth and Assets Survey.

### Is there a formula for expected net worth?

A well-known rough one from *The Millionaire Next Door* is age × pre-tax annual income ÷ 10. It is crude and breaks for young or newly high-earning people, but it is useful because it scales with both age and income.

### Should I compare to the average or the median net worth?

The median. Averages are pulled sharply upward by a small number of very wealthy people, so the average is much higher than what a typical person actually has.

### How do I calculate my own net worth?

Add up your assets (cash, investments, property, vehicles at current value) and subtract your liabilities (card balances, loans, mortgage). [Vexyn's Net Worth Analyzer](/net-worth-analyzer) does the arithmetic and keeps the figures on your device.

### Why is my net worth negative in my twenties?

That is common and usually fine — student loans or an early mortgage can outweigh savings for years. What matters is whether the figure trends upward over time, not its sign in a single snapshot.

### Where can I find trustworthy net worth data by age?

Primary sources: in the US, the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances; in the UK, the ONS Wealth and Assets Survey. Both are free, methodologically sound, and the ultimate source most articles quote.

## Glossary

**Net worth** — Everything you own (assets) minus everything you owe (liabilities). A snapshot of your financial position at a point in time.

**Median vs mean** — The median is the middle value (the typical person); the mean is the average, which a few very wealthy people inflate. For net worth, the median is the more honest benchmark.

**Expected net worth** — A rule-of-thumb estimate of what someone "should" have accumulated given their age and income, from *The Millionaire Next Door*: age × pre-tax income ÷ 10.

**Survey of Consumer Finances** — A US Federal Reserve study, run every few years, that publishes household wealth data including net worth by age. A primary source rather than a secondhand figure.
